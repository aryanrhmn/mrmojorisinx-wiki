Living Compilation: What Works / What Gets You Carried Out

I’ve worked in private equity, family offices and hedge funds. Analyst, PM, CIO. Seen a lot, learned a lot the hard way. I’ve seen what works, what I think hasn’t worked, what makes people last and get better, and also what gets them carried out.

There’s a lot of garbage advice on Twit. I really hope this isn’t that, and want to see you crush it.

Here are some things that I believe matter (will be a living compilation):
🧵

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 14:55:59 +0000 2025 · 1950933464942776653

These are principles that don’t expire. Some points will sound basic. Others you’ll only appreciate after the fact. Ignore what doesn’t fit. Steal what does.

(If you’ve read my posts like Everyone Has a Process, The Illusion of Readiness, Know Thyself, The Move I Never Learned...you’ll recognize some of this.)

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 14:56:56 +0000 2025 · 1950933703938490838

Show up early. Leave late. I’ve never seen a career blow up because someone was too available. I’ve seen plenty end because they weren’t.

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 14:59:54 +0000 2025 · 1950934451455770786

Every Friday, update your 'One pager' Summary / KPI sheet of your universe and supporting analysis. Take it home. Review it over the weekend. Then on Monday—leave the most important insight from the 'One Pager' you had for your PM (assuming no big news hit).

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:00:42 +0000 2025 · 1950934650966188091

Never miss a team meeting, portfolio review, or offsite. Even if the meeting gets rescheduled or cancelled, have the materials ready. Habits build trust.

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:01:40 +0000 2025 · 1950934896567783446

If you don’t understand something—fix it. Ask the risk team. Ask an analyst who’s been around longer. Sit with the traders. Read filings. Whatever it takes. Ignorance is expensive. (see: Know Thyself)

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:03:02 +0000 2025 · 1950935238172893333

Understand exposures and factors. Don’t stop at “I like the stock.” Know how factor moves, FX, rates and other variables impact your names that are in the portfolio and in your universe. Understand risk

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:04:12 +0000 2025 · 1950935534798344599

Always ask yourself when sizing and / or structuring a position:

Do you like the stock?
Do you like the sector?
Do you like the market?

Those three questions frame every hedge/position block/bundle.

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:05:02 +0000 2025 · 1950935742630281264

Talk to your trader(s), yes even the quant guy. Sit with them. Learn how position blocks work, how they think about exposure, and how liquidity really behaves. They hear (and some cases 'feel') things before you do.

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:06:38 +0000 2025 · 1950936144067076512

When a name you cover is involved in a corporate action, dive in—even if you’re out of the position (this also involves prioritization of workload).

Share it with the merger arb, event team, and credit team (if there is one). You’ll need that knowledge again.

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:07:24 +0000 2025 · 1950936337663562212

Track activist campaigns in your sector. Know the precedents and the players. If one shows up in your name, you should have an idea of what their playbook entails and likely course of action.

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:07:55 +0000 2025 · 1950936469867995495

Every name you cover should have a one-pager ready to print anytime.

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:09:12 +0000 2025 · 1950936790572970018

Don’t just “seek out mentors.”

Be an apprentice. Watch how the best do it. Ask sharp questions: “You mentioned X—can you walk me through that?”

(Read: The Illusion of Readiness and The Education That Paid Me)

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:10:21 +0000 2025 · 1950937080541946020

Print out your process. Turn it into a diagram. Learn to explain it.

Everyone has a process—even the people who think they don’t. Write yours down. Improve it over time.

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:11:16 +0000 2025 · 1950937311283220713

Go find analysts outside your coverage and soak up their space. Share how you each model. Learn what matters in their world re macro as well as idiosyncratic.

Make each other better.

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:12:30 +0000 2025 · 1950937624249549252

This is more for the young bucks:

Stop worrying about “balance.” There are 24 hours in a day. 6–8 for sleep. The rest? Be organized and make it count.

I guarantee you can fit it all in...

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:13:10 +0000 2025 · 1950937789366690200

Separate fact from opinion. If it’s a guess, say so. Don’t confuse “I think” with “I know.”

Conviction is the enemy

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:13:28 +0000 2025 · 1950937864432144578

Own mistakes fast. Fix them faster. People respect both. Hide them, and you won’t last.

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:14:04 +0000 2025 · 1950938015016329241

Don’t model to the decimal if your assumptions are soft. Direction first. Rate of change next. The slope matters more than the snapshot.

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:15:01 +0000 2025 · 1950938256129884483

Don’t gossip. Not about PMs, not about peers, and definitely not on Twitter. Pod shops know who their people are online (as do the big name big boy funds)

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:16:56 +0000 2025 · 1950938739603038468

Don’t show frustration on the trading floor or in front of others. People notice—and they remember.

The guy who slams his keyboard after a bad earnings print or is asked to stay late, or yells at the trader... you want to distance yourself from people like this

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:17:28 +0000 2025 · 1950938873099424087

Be responsive. Nights. Weekends. If something happens in your names, you need to be on it. Have a template ready to deliver news and updates.

@MrMojoRisinX · Thu Jul 31 15:18:33 +0000 2025 · 1950939144399589626

Read like crazy—books, filings, transcripts, investor letters. Judgment compounds the same way returns do: over years (not a couple of quarters).

Due diligence on a company or security is research. It is NOT Investment Process. Say it with me...Research is NOT process.

A sound process is the repeatable framework that governs everything: how you source your universe, how you conduct DD on a sector/company/security, sizing guidelines and/or rules, entry discipline (catalyst or no catalyst), what would make you change your mind and what you have to believe, portfolio construction, and risk management parameters.

Due dili is just one part of process.

Conflating the two is a mistake, especially when training people who are still forming habits.

Activism Full Circle: Pep Boys → Icahn → Real Estate Strip

Mojo ( @MrMojoRisinX ) · Tue Jul 21 12:52:50 +0000 2026
18 likes · 4 bookmarks · 4,330 views
Source file: History in shareholder activism comes full circle.html
https://x.com/MrMojoRisinX/status/2079550165728297442
History in shareholder activism comes full circle:

A decade ago, activists like Steel Partners, Barington, Ramius, Pirate Capital, & Gabelli pushed Pep Boys to cut costs, take on leverage, and seek "strategic alternatives."

Carl Icahn ultimately wins the bidding war, buying it for $1.03B in 2016. Fast forward to today: Icahn Enterprises sells the auto-service operations to Mavis for $700M while pocketing and retaining the underlying real estate.

Most activist playbooks are just short-term trades disguised as long-term investments, relying heavily on aggressive financial engineering.

If corporate collaboration agreements aren't buttoned up tightly, this pattern of hollowed-out corporate value destruction will keep repeating.

There is a time for pressure cooker shareholder activism that leads with financial engineering, but most of the time it is better to work constructively with C-suites and boards to effectuate positive change that doesn't lead with short-term cost cuts, buybacks, asset disposals, and sell-the-company agendas.

Quoting Paul Nary @ProfPaulNary
Activism good or activism bad?
I talk to a lot of activist investors, and my perception is that the activism game today is very different from what it was ten, much less twenty years ago.

So I don't know if I'd agree with Mojo, who generally has great insights, that the playbook is the same today, except for maybe some stubborn holdovers clinging to the past. And much of the "good" or constructive activist work is often done in private rather than public campaigns, so perhaps it is less noticeable and often undisclosed.

Either way, I think it's hard to deny that today it's harder to run and win proxy battles as an activist, it's harder to succeed with shorter-term focused approaches and hostile tactics, and the public firms today are much better prepared to deal with activist attacks.

And although some funds still remain more on the aggressive/public/short-term side, or at least keep those strategies in their pockets, the smarter/more successful or at least consistent ones seem to tend to do things differently, again, based on my conversations:

1) They often favor private, constructive approaches, only resorting to public campaigns when absolutely necessary.

2) They seem to focus on their own competences and what they can bring to the table, pragmatically, and do their homework with a focus on trying to understand the most critical factors relevant to their own strategy/situational assessment.

3) They are more likely to prioritize collaboration over control, e.g. don't necessarily need a board seat if the management works well with you.

4) They tend to be more long-term oriented, although balance may matter here and it can work against you. I've personally observed and discussed more than one case with activists who were too patient and permissive and let management get away with doing the obviously wrong thing for too long, even to the degree that it was too late in a few cases.

And by the way, I think we need more activism today than ever, because as activists became somewhat less of a threat in today's environment, combined with the rise of passives and other factors, managers and boards are now subject to fewer disciplining mechanisms, and more prone to get away with destruction of shareholder value, extraction of rents, etc. And even the CEOs I talk to (especially the ones who have retired/stepped down) will often admit that while some activists and campaigns are obviously purely opportunistic, poorly thought-out, and short-term focused on rent extraction, there are activists out there that come to firms for the right reasons and help them do the right thing.

Now of course, again, my opinion is that we need more of the type of activism that takes a thoughtful, constructive approach that ultimately benefits the shareholders and does not destroy the long-term value of businesses.

By the way, the last three decades of academic research do seem to generally imply, at least in my own reading, that on average, activisits have a positive influence and improve firms and increase shareholder value through a variety of mechanisms, but that's a much more complex conversation.
