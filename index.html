Starving for Process: Sourcing, Universe, Origination

Mojo ( @MrMojoRisinX ) · Sun Jan 11 15:34:23 +0000 2026
415 likes · 19 reposts · 499 bookmarks · 10 replies · 117,169 views
Source file: Twitter is drowning in stock picks while starving for investment process. The issue is Twitter.html
https://x.com/MrMojoRisinX/status/2010374737545863639
Twitter is drowning in stock picks while starving for investment process. The issue is Twitter doesn't realize this. People haven't gone through the process of knowing thyself or even really digging in deep to execute their PMs process better, versus criticizing them in public after the PM wants to size the position larger after the stock has moved, when in reality the market provided information the PM "needed to see" in order to feel more confident.

I see so many isolated questions on Twitter that are impossible to answer without more context. How many positions should I own? Should I use stops? How long should I hold? Anyone dealing in absolutes here isn't helping you. The only honest answer is "it depends." To actually help someone/team, I need to understand how they invest, their mandate, their role within it. What I don't see enough of: How did you source this and does it fit your universe? How do you size it, structure it, monitor it, and manage the risk? What drives your exits? Valuation, thesis deterioration, news, or just emotional reaction to price? Is your process actually driving decisions or are you just reacting?

Investment process doesn't start with due diligence checklists or valuation multiples. It starts with sourcing and origination. This is where people need to spend a lot more time. Define your universe. Rank order it. Understand your mandate and your role within it.

I'm a believer in direction of fundamentals over snapshot multiples. Force ranking by valuation alone will make you struggle. Valuation should be an observed statistic, not your primary sorting mechanism. This comes from years building process across different fund structures: macro managers, short sellers paid on absolute returns and some paid purely on alpha, value investors needing complete process overhauls, activist situations requiring engagement strategy rather than underwriting help.

People hate when I say this, but I don't need to know everything about your position to strengthen or weaken the thesis. Part of why this is true is because of portfolio construction. Portfolios aren't just a collection of trades but a holistic expression of mosaic, conviction, risk management, and opportunity cost. When someone brings an idea to investment committee or reaches out to discuss one-on-one, whatever the backdrop, they'll always know more details (not drivers) than me. That doesn't make my questions less valuable. I prefer getting involved when situations require deeper thinking: direct engagement with management, analyzing incentive structures, pushing for change. But none of this matters without proper sourcing and origination.

If you find an interesting idea from reading Twitter, talking to a hedge fund contact, or hearing from sell-side (and then complaining about the price target change after news), really think about whether it belongs in your universe.

People criticize the pod structure, but pods have repeatable process: drawdown triggers book cuts, position-level stops, hard sizing guidelines, exposure parameters. It starts with defining the universe and rank ordering from there. I'm not a pod guy. Spent time there and think it's valuable training ground for building multi-step process, but it's not my desired way to invest. The discipline matters more than the structure.

Someone will invoke Tepper's gut or Druck's instincts. You don't know Tepper and you don't know Druck. They're anomalies like Buffett who've evolved over decades as markets changed. Tepper's brass balls are about sizing, not sourcing. Never borrow someone else's conviction. (See my $CFLT dark arts post.)

Unfortunately, putting this all together, isn't in some book. It's the Good Will Hunting scene where Robin Williams challenges Will about real experience versus reading. You can study investment theory, but building actual process comes from years across different strategies, learning markets and yourself, extensive trial and error. I've written about this extensively at Inside the Mind of Mojo: pieces like "Everyone Has a Process," the merger arbitrage frameworks, the multiflation archetypes.

I implore you to focus more on sourcing and origination. Define your universe and rank order it! It will improve your process, your hit rate, reduce drawdowns, and position you to play better offense during dislocations.

Due diligence on a company or security is research. It is NOT Investment Process. Say it with me...Research is NOT process.

A sound process is the repeatable framework that governs everything: how you source your universe, how you conduct DD on a sector/company/security, sizing guidelines and/or rules, entry discipline (catalyst or no catalyst), what would make you change your mind and what you have to believe, portfolio construction, and risk management parameters.

Due dili is just one part of process.

Conflating the two is a mistake, especially when training people who are still forming habits.

What You Have to Believe (Plants Edition $MSOS) - Feel free to add to this list and bring some positive energy!

#WYHTB

This is not about buying snapshot cheap multiples (today). This is about the rate of change of fundamentals and being able to look at plant stocks with normalized income statements, kosher tax treatment, properly capitalized balance sheets and what that looks like on the other side.

You have to believe in the plant itself. This is not about marijuana. It's not about weed. It aint about hemp. This is about plants. The medicinal and recreational benefits for adults, seniors, people in pain, people who have run out of options or are simply looking for a better way to live.
 
You have to believe the senior demographic is one of the sleeper demand drivers. Boomers in pain with disposable income and who are done with opioids. But you also have to believe the doctors get there too (based on my experience, we gotta lot of work to do - Cleveland Clinic, Johns Hopkins, Mayo Clinic). Currently, there are too many physicians that are still pushing pills because the incentive structure from big pharma makes it easier than having an honest conversation. This entire mindset has to change!

You have to believe the White House is not only paying attention (which they clearly are), but they aren't going to stop until the job is done (don't rest in the middle...rest when it's over). One in five adults used hemp derived CBD in the past year. Trump signed an Executive Order on hemp derived CBD research and innovation in December. Dr. Oz is already moving on it with a senior focused model and DJT is calling on Congress to act fast. Read the god damn room.

You have to believe the illicit market continues to get cleaned up at the local level (my sources tell me the number of busts in the south/east coast are about to get real busy...). States moving toward medical legalization need to look hard at what is happening inside smoke shops right now. Kratom being sold while veterans sit in the parking lot with their kids in the car. High schoolers hanging outside waiting for an adult to buy for them. These places need surveillance. They need to be cleaned out. 

You have to believe in a version of the STATES Act (this is going to get those phonies super fired up), but Federal rescheduling is the floor. The real prize is a framework that respects state sovereignty, creates legit free markets, and does not hand the keys to a handful of players who have been lobbying in the dark to build a moat around themselves. Follow the money. Then follow the people who actually want free markets and real capitalism in this space.

This one is tough...but You have to believe the DEA finds the right lane right now. Quality control. Consumer protection. Keeping kids out of it. No picking winners and losers. Not creating new regulatory bottlenecks or loopholes for the next generation of bad actors to exploit.

You have to believe it may not look like alcohol (regulation) now or even ever. But that the space has been properly regulated for the most part and is a reasonable framework. 

And look. There is just too much anger in this space. If you have nothing to contribute, eat an edible, hit a spliff, something. And lay off the weekly $MSOS options trades. 

You have to believe the uplist happens. Real conversations have been in motion for months. Trust but verify. The mechanics are being worked through right now at various levels including the exchanges.

You have to believe the institutional bid shows up. Every larger cap C-suite is getting an audience right now with the Blackrocks and Fidelitys of the world. They are learning more about the space as well as the names. And trying to better understand the operating models. So that when the regulatory mechanism clears they can move.

You have to believe hedge funds are quietly positioning in select names, waiting for liquidity to improve as well as working through incorporating the sector exposures into risk parameters of their mandates and how to communicate this to limited partners. 

You have to believe the fuckery in the ETF ecosystem softens. The swap structure, the daily smash algo, the daily extraction. Upon rescheduling and uplisting, market forces find true price discovery. 

You have to believe reinvestment produces higher ROIC. The companies that take capital freed up by lower cost debt, 280e going bye-bye and operational efficiency and put it back into the business will generate returns that justify the multiple expansion.

You have to believe the high cost indoor producer model finally breaks somewhere. Power costs in the US are rising. In Europe they are already prohibitive, and in Canada its starting to become a problem albeit under discussed for obvious reasons. At least one large player makes that call and partners with a major biomass producer.

You have to believe consolidation makes the sector structurally stronger. Mergers between large players. A best in class operator executing a global rollup, shutting down expensive production, building synergistic distribution ecosystems. JVs between plant operators and consumer and medical players. Capital injections to help companies grow!

You have to believe branded alcohol and cannabis partnerships come to fruition. There are a lot of RFPs in motion...this much I promise you. Along the same lines You have to believe the consumer trade from alcohol to cannabis is structural. The beverage data is showing it.

You have to believe that companies building research partnerships today are building something pure retail operators cannot replicate. Do some dili on what $CURL is doing with UPenn and $GLASF is doing with UC Berkeley.

You have to believe in companies building distribution and partnership ecosystems right now. The ones creating real commercial infrastructure with complementary players are going to look very different from the lone wolves when the market opens up.

You have to believe C-suites are going to bring the energy and commit to the next four months of roadshows. The best management teams do not view this as a task. They just love talking about their businesses.
Hint: follow the Incentive Compensation structures are changing. 

You have to believe the export market opens. US grown product moving to Europe and Asia by 2027.
You have to believe restricted markets open. Great Britain is the one worth watching as patients who need access cannot get it right now without a lot of effort!

Lastly, You have to believe that We have the ability to let the past go and look forward to the future.

Activism Full Circle: Pep Boys → Icahn → Real Estate Strip

History in shareholder activism comes full circle:

A decade ago, activists like Steel Partners, Barington, Ramius, Pirate Capital, & Gabelli pushed Pep Boys to cut costs, take on leverage, and seek "strategic alternatives."

Carl Icahn ultimately wins the bidding war, buying it for $1.03B in 2016. Fast forward to today: Icahn Enterprises sells the auto-service operations to Mavis for $700M while pocketing and retaining the underlying real estate.

Most activist playbooks are just short-term trades disguised as long-term investments, relying heavily on aggressive financial engineering.

If corporate collaboration agreements aren't buttoned up tightly, this pattern of hollowed-out corporate value destruction will keep repeating.

There is a time for pressure cooker shareholder activism that leads with financial engineering, but most of the time it is better to work constructively with C-suites and boards to effectuate positive change that doesn't lead with short-term cost cuts, buybacks, asset disposals, and sell-the-company agendas.

Activism good or activism bad?
I talk to a lot of activist investors, and my perception is that the activism game today is very different from what it was ten, much less twenty years ago.

So I don't know if I'd agree with Mojo, who generally has great insights, that the playbook is the same today, except for maybe some stubborn holdovers clinging to the past. And much of the "good" or constructive activist work is often done in private rather than public campaigns, so perhaps it is less noticeable and often undisclosed.

Either way, I think it's hard to deny that today it's harder to run and win proxy battles as an activist, it's harder to succeed with shorter-term focused approaches and hostile tactics, and the public firms today are much better prepared to deal with activist attacks.

And although some funds still remain more on the aggressive/public/short-term side, or at least keep those strategies in their pockets, the smarter/more successful or at least consistent ones seem to tend to do things differently, again, based on my conversations:

1) They often favor private, constructive approaches, only resorting to public campaigns when absolutely necessary.

2) They seem to focus on their own competences and what they can bring to the table, pragmatically, and do their homework with a focus on trying to understand the most critical factors relevant to their own strategy/situational assessment.

3) They are more likely to prioritize collaboration over control, e.g. don't necessarily need a board seat if the management works well with you.

4) They tend to be more long-term oriented, although balance may matter here and it can work against you. I've personally observed and discussed more than one case with activists who were too patient and permissive and let management get away with doing the obviously wrong thing for too long, even to the degree that it was too late in a few cases.

And by the way, I think we need more activism today than ever, because as activists became somewhat less of a threat in today's environment, combined with the rise of passives and other factors, managers and boards are now subject to fewer disciplining mechanisms, and more prone to get away with destruction of shareholder value, extraction of rents, etc. And even the CEOs I talk to (especially the ones who have retired/stepped down) will often admit that while some activists and campaigns are obviously purely opportunistic, poorly thought-out, and short-term focused on rent extraction, there are activists out there that come to firms for the right reasons and help them do the right thing.

Now of course, again, my opinion is that we need more of the type of activism that takes a thoughtful, constructive approach that ultimately benefits the shareholders and does not destroy the long-term value of businesses.

By the way, the last three decades of academic research do seem to generally imply, at least in my own reading, that on average, activisits have a positive influence and improve firms and increase shareholder value through a variety of mechanisms, but that's a much more complex conversation.
